Start with the question, not the chart

A chart is finished when a reader can say what it means and what to do about it, without you in the room.

Before you open a tool, write the question in one sentence: "Where is attrition concentrated?", not "show attrition".

Then pick the mode. Explanatory charts prove a finding, for decks and reports (the default). Exploratory views let readers find answers, for dashboards. Both follow the same rules.

Both charts here are accurate; only one is useful in a meeting.

How readers read a chart

Readers move through a chart in three steps. Design for each one, in order.

1 Perceive

In the first second the eye takes in shape, length and color before reading a word. Make the thing that matters the thing that stands out: one color against grey.

2 Interpret

Next the reader looks for meaning: headline, labels, units, benchmarks. The headline should confirm what they just saw, or sharpen it.

3 Act

Finally they ask "so what?". A callout that sizes the implication turns a finding into a decision.

Fundamentals

Anatomy of a chart

Every McKesson chart has the same four layers, top to bottom: headline, subtitle, chart, and footnote with source.

The headline and subtitle do different jobs. The headline is editorial: it states the finding. The subtitle is neutral: metric, unit, scope and period.

Inside the chart, everything is labelled in place, so the reader never has to match colors to a legend.

All four layers share one left edge. In HTML they are page text; the chart library draws only the data.

  1. 1Insight headlineA full sentence with the number. At most two lines, no final period.
  2. 2SubtitleMetric, unit, scope and period. No opinions.
  3. 3Direct labelsSeries named at the line end, in the series color. No legend.
  4. 4Focus and contextThe series the headline is about is blue; the comparison is grey.
  5. 5Forecast markedA shaded period and dashed lines separate estimates from actuals.
  6. 6Range, not a pointForecasts show their uncertainty.
  7. 7The point, annotatedThe gap the headline quotes, labelled where it is.
  8. 8Light scaffoldingFew, light gridlines; units live in the subtitle.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefinitions, system, as-of date, population.

Fundamentals

Write the headline last, from the numbers

The headline is the most-read text on the page. It should be the finding, not the topic.

The formula

Who or what + verb + number + so what.

Before you keep it

  • Recompute every number, "all", "most" and "n of m" from the data
  • The blue in the chart is what the headline names
  • Two lines at most; no final period

If you can't write a headline, you don't have a finding yet: you have a chart.

Attrition by job family · names the topic; says nothing
Vague
Attrition varies across job families · true of every chart ever made

Fundamentals

Grey first, then color the point

Color is for meaning, never decoration. Start with every mark in grey, then color only what the headline names.

Highlight only what the headline names, usually one or two items. When everything is highlighted, nothing is.

Echo the highlight in the text: bold the matching category label, and set callouts in the color of the mark they describe.

Orange means "worse". Keep it for negatives: below target, shrinking, a shortfall. Never use it as a plain highlight; readers take it as a warning.

Fundamentals

Type and proportion

One typeface, Libre Franklin, in a few fixed roles. Type should frame the data, not compete with it.

The headline is the largest text on the chart but the data is the largest thing. If the headline is the first thing you notice from across the room, it is too heavy.

  • Nothing below 9 pt on slides, 11 px on screens
  • Sentence case everywhere; no all-caps headlines
  • Headline, subtitle, key, footnote and source share one left edge
  • Slide titles Bold; a chart's own headline SemiBold
  • Big numbers Bold, so they pop

Libre Franklin is open source (SIL OFL). Set-up for Office, the web and Power BI is in the Toolkit.

{{html:type_specimen}}

Proportion on a 16:9 slide

Margins of about 0.5 in. The chart takes at least 60% of the slide height. One message per chart, at most two charts per slide. With one chart, the slide title is its headline; with two, each chart gets its own SemiBold insight headline (see Formats).

Proportion on a screen

The chart card sits on a quiet background with generous padding. Headline about 1.4× the subtitle. On phones, swap end labels for a top key and let wide exhibits scroll sideways rather than shrink.

Fundamentals

Label directly, delete the rest

Every element must earn its place. If removing it loses nothing, remove it.

  1. Replace the label-style title with an insight headline
  2. Sort by value, not alphabet
  3. Turn long-named columns into horizontal bars
  4. Grey everything, then color the point
  5. Label values in place; delete the value axis, gridlines and tick marks
  6. Delete the legend, borders and background fill
  7. Add the benchmark, labelled where it sits

Honest scales

A chart can be accurate in every number and still mislead. These four rules prevent the common ways.

Bars start at zero. If the differences only show on a narrow range, change the chart, not the axis.

No dual axes. Two scales let you make any two lines cross wherever you like.

Bubble area, not diameter, is proportional to value.

Pies take five slices at most, and sum to 100%.

The one exception: a waterfall bridging two large totals may break its axis, if the break is drawn and footnoted.

Fundamentals

People data is personal

Workforce charts describe colleagues, and carry extra obligations.

Minimum group size

No group under {{text:min_n}} people: not in bars, labels, tooltips, filters or drill-downs. Merge into "Other" and footnote it.

No re-identification

Small team × tenure × location can single out one person, even when each cut is large.

Protected attributes and forecasts

Protected attributes only for approved analyses such as pay equity, aggregated. Label estimates and show a range. Frame findings about roles, not individuals.

Engagement by team, one distribution center

Two teams have fewer than {{text:min_n}} respondents: anyone who knows the team can guess who answered what.

A rule in a slide is easy to forget; a rule in the measure can't be skipped. In Power BI: IF([Headcount] < {{text:min_n}}, BLANK(), [Engagement]). In Python, drop or merge small groups before plotting, and keep individual values out of hover text.

Fundamentals

Accessible by default

About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a color-vision deficiency. In a room of 25, assume someone does.

  • Text contrast at least 4.5:1
  • No red vs. green in data; red, amber and green only as status colors, each with its symbol and word
  • Never color alone: pair it with labels, position or shape
  • Nothing below 9 pt on slides or 11 px on screens
  • Alt text for every chart that leaves the team: the headline plus two or three key numbers

The red–green examples are simulated for deuteranopia, the most common form.
