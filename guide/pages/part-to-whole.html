Part-to-whole

Stacked bar chart

Part-to-whole charts show how a whole breaks into its parts, and how each part relates to the total.

A 100% stacked bar compares the mix of several wholes at once. Color only the segment the headline is about, and put it on the baseline so every bar's value starts level.

McKesson spec

  • Five segments at most; group the rest as "Other"
  • The story segment in blue, on the baseline
  • Values inside segments that fit; names over the top bar
  • Show each bar's base: 100% hides size
  1. 1Insight headlineNames the part and how far it stands out.
  2. 2SubtitleMetric, unit, scope, period.
  3. 3The part that mattersBlue, on the baseline, so every bar compares from zero.
  4. 4Grey contextTwo greys, split by white separators.
  5. 5Values in the segmentsWhere they fit; white on blue, navy on grey.
  6. 6Names, not a keyEach segment named once, over the top bar.
  7. 7The baseA 100% bar hides how big each whole is; give the count.
  8. 8Sorted by the storyBars ordered by the blue segment, not alphabetically.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine the parts.

Stacked bar variants

Same rules: one part in color, the rest grey, labels in place.

When a stack is the wrong chart

  • One whole with up to five parts: a donut or a single bar
  • How a total changes step by step: a waterfall
  • Many parts or a hierarchy: a treemap
  • Comparing the parts themselves across groups: grouped bars or small multiples; only the baseline segment of a stack compares cleanly

Stacked bar best practice

Stacks are easy to make and easy to misread. Four rules keep them honest.

The part that matters on the baseline. Only segments that share a starting line can be compared by eye.

Five segments at most. More slices turn a bar into a barcode. Keep the parts the story needs and group the rest.

Label in place. Values inside the segments; name each segment once over the top bar. If a key is unavoidable, top-left, in stack order.

One order in every bar. Never re-sort segments within each bar.

Waterfall chart

A waterfall shows how a starting total becomes an ending total, one driver at a time.

It is a stacked column chart with an invisible spacer series that floats each driver at the running total. Use it for labor cost bridges, headcount bridges and budget-to-actual walks.

McKesson spec

  • Totals in navy; drivers grey, the ones the headline names in blue
  • Or by meaning: favorable green, unfavorable orange
  • Thin light connectors between bars
  • Signed labels on drivers (true minus, explicit +); bold totals
  • If the axis starts above zero, mark the break and say so
  1. 1Insight headlineSizes what the named drivers did to the total.
  2. 2SubtitleFrom and to, with the unit.
  3. 3Totals in navyStart, end and any subtotal.
  4. 4Marked breakThe axis starts at $3,900M; white slashes say so.
  5. 5The drivers that matterIn blue; label bold.
  6. 6Thin connectorsLight grey, so the bars stay dominant.
  7. 7Signed labels+139, −58: direction without color.
  8. 8Grey contextEvery other driver, in logical order.
  9. 9FootnoteStates the axis break.

The same build, turned or recolored for the job.

Part-to-whole

Waterfall best practice

The bars carry the story; everything else stays quiet.

Thin connectors. Heavy dark connectors compete with the bars. Use hairlines in light grey.

Color by meaning, in the palette. Favorable green, unfavorable orange, judged from the reader's view: in a cost bridge, growth is the bad news. Never red and green: they fail for color-blind readers, and green-up makes cost growth look good.

Mark a broken axis. Totals often dwarf the drivers. Starting the axis above zero is allowed here, and only here, if the break is drawn and footnoted.

Group the small steps. Keep the drivers the story needs; fold the rest together.

Treemap

Nested rectangles whose area is proportional to value. A treemap shows many parts, or a hierarchy, in one compact block.

Use it when there are too many parts for a donut or a stack (ten to thirty roles, sites or skills) and the point is which few dominate.

Don't use it when readers need to compare similar sizes precisely, or there are only a handful of parts: a sorted bar is clearer.

McKesson spec

  • Squarified layout, largest top-left
  • Thin, even white gutters
  • Grey tiles; the ones the headline names in blue
  • Name and value inside each tile that fits
  1. 1Insight headlineShare of the whole held by the named few.
  2. 2SubtitleWhat area measures, with the unit.
  3. 3Focus in blueThe two roles the headline names.
  4. 4Grey contextEvery other role, the same grey.
  5. 5Smallest lastBottom-right. Tiles too small for a label get none; list them in a footnote if they matter.
  6. 6Largest firstTop-left, flowing to the smallest; labels top-left inside each tile.
  7. 7Footnote and sourceDefine the measure.

Part-to-whole

Treemap best practice

A treemap works when it reads as one shape that flows from large to small.

Largest to smallest. Don't arrange tiles alphabetically or by an org grouping that breaks the flow.

Labels inside. No label bands or boxes over the tiles; they distort the areas. If a tile can't hold its label, leave it unlabelled or label it outside.

Even padding. Uneven gaps make similar tiles look different.

Few parts? Use a bar. Areas are harder to compare than lengths.

Donut and pie

A donut shows one whole split into a few parts. Prefer it to a pie: readers follow the arc length around the ring, and the center holds the total.

Use it for one whole and at most five parts. To compare several wholes, use 100% stacked bars; for more parts, a bar or treemap.

McKesson spec

  • Five segments at most; "Other" last
  • Largest first, clockwise from 12 o'clock
  • Hole about 60% of the diameter; white separators
  • Labels and values outside; no legend
  1. 1Insight headlineThe share, and what it means for the plan.
  2. 2SubtitleThe whole, the unit, the year.
  3. 3Start at 12Largest first, then clockwise.
  4. 4The part that mattersIn blue; the rest grey.
  5. 5Total in the centerBold, large.
  6. 6Labels outsideName and share, beside each segment.
  7. 7White separatorsThin gaps keep segments distinct.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceDefine the parts.

Part-to-whole

Donut best practice

People judge length faster than angle or area, which is why the donut beats the pie.

Donut over pie. The ring turns angles into arc lengths, and the hole holds the total.

Labels outside. Squeezed labels on the ring are hard to read; put names and values outside.

Few segments. Group the smallest into "Other".

Largest first, clockwise from 12. Don't mix small and large at random.

Progress rings and dials for a single percentage are in Single number.

Build part-to-whole charts

Every tool can make these natively. The settings below are the ones that matter; tested code and click-by-click steps are on the cookbook pages.

stacked-bar.md · waterfall.md · treemap.md · donut-pie.md

Before you send it

  • Parts sum to the whole (100%, or the end total)
  • Five parts at most; "Other" last
  • One part in color, matching the headline
  • Waterfall axis break marked and footnoted
  • No group under {{text:min_n}} people, in any part
PowerPoint and Excel

Stacked: Stacked or 100% Stacked Bar, Gap Width 50%, Overlap 100%, white 1.5 pt borders; move the story series first or last in Select Data. Waterfall: Insert › Waterfall, then right-click each total › Set as Total; drivers grey, totals navy. Treemap: Insert › Hierarchy › Treemap; labels Overlapping, not Banner. Donut: Doughnut, angle of first slice 0°, hole 62%, data sorted descending.

Power BI

Native 100% stacked bar, Waterfall, Treemap and Donut visuals all take the theme. Set the story color per series or with a field-value color measure; legend top-left or off; data labels on. The native waterfall can't color single bars or break the axis: use a stacked column with a transparent spacer measure when you need that.

Python

ax.barh(..., left=running_total, edgecolor="white") for stacks; the waterfall, squarified treemap and donut recipes in the cookbook use only matplotlib and mckviz. Plotly has native go.Waterfall, go.Treemap and go.Pie(hole=0.6).

colors = mv.highlight(parts, focus="Contingent")          # one part blue, rest grey
mv.lint(fig)    # flags > 6 colors, legends, and bars that don't start at zero
HTML

Plotly with MCK_PLOTLY_TEMPLATE: barmode: "stack", waterfall and treemap traces. Read colors from var(--mck-chart-focus) and var(--mck-chart-context).

R

geom_col(position = "fill") for 100% stacks; treemapify::geom_treemap() for treemaps; waterfalls with geom_rect(). Colors from mck_highlight().
