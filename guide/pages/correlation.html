Bubble scatterplot

Correlation charts show how two or more measures relate to each other: does X go with Y, and who sits where it matters?

Each entity is one mark. Position carries two measures; bubble area can carry a third, such as headcount. Use it for exposure vs. pay by role, engagement vs. span of control by team, or time to fill vs. market tightness by metro.

McKesson spec

  • Grey bubbles; the ones the headline names in blue
  • Bubble area, not diameter, ∝ value; a size key
  • Label five points at most; shade the zone that matters
  • Say "is associated with", never "causes"
  1. 1Insight headlineWho is in the zone, and by how much.
  2. 2SubtitleBoth measures, the period, and what bubble area means.
  3. 3The zone that mattersShaded behind the marks and named in place.
  4. 4Focus in blue, labelledOnly the bubbles the headline names.
  5. 5Grey contextUnlabelled; big bubbles drawn first, white outlines.
  6. 6Reference lineNavy, dashed, its value computed and labelled.
  7. 7Size keyThree round values on the same scale.
  8. 8Horizontal axis titlesUnits on the axes; the Y title top-left.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine the measure and the average.

Scatterplot variants

The same rules hold: grey context, blue for the story, labels only where the headline points.

When a scatter is the wrong chart

  • One measure, ranked: a bar
  • One measure over time: a line
  • The X measure is a category: a dot plot
  • Thousands of overlapping people: a binned heatmap
  • Four or more measures per entity: parallel coordinates

Scatterplot best practice

A scatter invites the eye to roam. These four rules give it somewhere to land.

Color carries a variable, or the story. Coloring by x-range repeats the position and encodes nothing.

Two trend lines at most. One line per highlighted group; the rest stay grey, with no line.

Label what the headline names. Twelve labels bury the point. Put the rest in tooltips.

Big bubbles behind. Draw the largest first, with white outlines, so small ones stay visible.

Dual axis charts

Two measures in different units, one axis each. Both ranges are arbitrary, so where the lines cross or track is the designer's choice, not the data's.

Pick the alternative from the question

  • Did B move when A moved? Two aligned panels on one time axis
  • Which grew faster? Index both to 100 at a stated base
  • How did B respond to A? A connected scatter

If a template forces one: columns for the quantity, a line for the rate, both axes from zero, no horizontal gridlines, each axis title colored to its series.

Diverging heatmap

A heatmap shows one measure across two sets of categories as color, so the eye finds patterns in 30 to 150 cells at once: a weak column, a strong row, one hot cell.

Use a diverging scale when the data has a meaningful middle: a benchmark, a target, change vs. last year. Use a sequential scale when the reference sits at one end, such as zero.

McKesson spec

  • Seven even classes; the middle one Light Sand
  • Orange below the reference (worse), blue above
  • Rows ranked by value, never alphabetical
  • Outline one column or row; don't add a hue
  1. 1Insight headlineThe pattern, counted from the data.
  2. 2SubtitleMeasure, reference, scope, unit.
  3. 3Neutral middleSymmetric classes around zero, stepped key with end labels.
  4. 4Focus columnOutlined in navy, header bold.
  5. 5Ranked rowsBy average gap, so the pattern emerges.
  6. 6Orange means worseBelow the benchmark, darker the further below.
  7. 7Values in the cellsWhite on dark fills, navy on light.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceDefine the gap; note any suppression.

Heatmap variants

Same build: white gutters, even classes, ranked rows. Blue is the default ramp for any single measure.

When a heatmap is the wrong chart

  • A few values to compare precisely: a bar or dot plot
  • A trend for one to four series: a line
  • Columns in different units: a table, or one scale per column
  • Places: a map

Heatmap best practice

Color is read less precisely than length, so the scale has to be honest and the order has to help.

Even steps. Hand-picked breaks (1, 2, 4, 10, 25) make small differences look large. Use five to seven equal classes.

Rank the rows. Alphabetical order scatters the pattern; ranking by average reveals it.

Keep the zero step. A diverging scale without a neutral class forces every cell above or below.

No cell under {{text:min_n}} people. Grey it, mark it n<10 and footnote it, in tooltips too.

Before you send it

  • Bubble area ∝ value, with a size key
  • Only the headline's points labelled
  • Association, not cause, in the headline
  • No dual axis: panels or an index
  • Diverging midpoint = the real reference
  • No point or cell under {{text:min_n}} people
PowerPoint and Excel

Bubble: Insert › Insert Scatter (X, Y) or Bubble Chart › Bubble; split focus and context into two series; Size represents: Area of bubbles; label the focus series with Value From Cells. Instead of a dual axis: an index helper column =B2/B$2*100, or two line charts with identical width, aligned left. Heatmap: Conditional Formatting › Color Scales › More Rules › 3-Color Scale with fixed Number bounds (−10.5, 0, 10.5), or one rule per class; white cell borders as gutters.

Power BI

Scatter chart with the entity in Values; color by measure through Markers › Colors › fx; averages and thresholds as Analytics-pane constant lines. Matrix with Cell elements › Background color › fx (Gradient with a middle color, or Rules per class). Index measures replace a dual axis. Blank small groups: IF([Headcount] < 10, BLANK(), …).

Python

matplotlib with mckviz; stepped classes from BoundaryNorm.

ax.scatter(df.pay, df.exposure, s=df.headcount * K,      # area ∝ value
           c=mv.highlight(df.role, focus=focus), edgecolors="white")
norm = BoundaryNorm(np.arange(-10.5, 10.6, 3), 7)         # 7 even classes, 0 in the middle
ax.imshow(m, cmap=ListedColormap(mv.DIVERGING), norm=norm)
HTML

Plotly scatter with sizemode: "area" and tooltips for the unlabelled points; heatmap with a stepped colorscale and xgap/ygap 2; make_subplots(shared_xaxes=True) for aligned panels. Avoid secondary_y.

R

geom_point(aes(size = headcount)) + scale_size_area(); geom_tile() + scale_fill_mck_div(limits = c(-10.5, 10.5)); facet_wrap(~ measure, ncol = 1, scales = "free_y") for aligned panels.
