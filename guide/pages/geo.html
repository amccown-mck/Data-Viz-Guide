Geo-spatial

Choropleth map

Geo-spatial charts show where: a pattern across geography, or values at precise locations.

A choropleth shades each area by a rate: postings per candidate, attrition, share eligible to retire. Use it when the pattern only makes sense on a map.

US maps use an equal-area Albers projection, with Alaska and Hawaii moved into insets.

McKesson spec

  • Sequential blue in 3–5 even classes
  • Light base, thin white boundaries
  • Label only the areas the headline names
  1. 1Insight headlineNames the states and the threshold.
  2. 2SubtitleA rate, with its unit, scope and period.
  3. 3Equal-area projectionAlbers: each state at its true relative size.
  4. 4InsetsAlaska shrunk and moved, Hawaii moved; both keep their color.
  5. 5Classed keyFive even classes, open-ended, high on top.
  6. 6Values on the fewOnly the 1.7+ states carry a number.
  7. 7Quiet structureThin white borders; no frame, no graticule.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceDefine the rate and the classes.

Bubble map

Bubbles on a quiet base map show counts at locations: headcount by site, open requisitions by metro, hires by campus.

Bubble area carries the count. Color can carry a second measure with the focus rule: blue for the sites the headline names, grey for the rest.

McKesson spec

  • Area ∝ value; largest drawn first, white outlines
  • Largest bubble about 40–56 px across
  • Label the focus sites: bold value, then name
  • Drop sites under {{text:min_n}} people and footnote them
  1. 1Insight headlineThe sites, the threshold, what's at stake.
  2. 2SubtitleSays what area and color mean.
  3. 3Area = associatesScaled by area, never radius.
  4. 4Focus in blueThe sites at 12%+ vacancy.
  5. 5Direct labelsBold value and site name, for the focus only.
  6. 6Grey contextEvery other site; overlaps stay readable.
  7. 7Size and color keyTwo reference circles, two swatches.
  8. 8Quiet baseSand states, white borders: the map is context.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine vacancy; note what's suppressed.

World map

For global questions: where to source talent, where a capability center could go, where our people work.

Use an equal-area projection such as Equal Earth. Mercator inflates Canada, Russia and Greenland and shrinks India and Africa, so a rate looks bigger the further it is from the equator.

McKesson spec

  • Equal Earth (or Robinson where a tool offers no equal-area option); Antarctica left out
  • Countries without data in base sand, and keyed
  • No palest class: it would read as "no data"
  • Rates as shades; counts as bubbles
  1. 1Insight headlineThe standout country, compared with home.
  2. 2SubtitleA rate, the countries in scope, the period.
  3. 3Equal EarthTrue relative areas; curved edges, no frame.
  4. 4Not assessedBase sand, so missing never looks like low.
  5. 5Classed keyFour even classes plus "Not assessed".
  6. 6The standoutNamed, with its value, in place.
  7. 7The comparisonThe U.S. value the headline uses.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceDefine the rate and the scope.

Zoom-in map

When the detail is smaller than a state (counties, a metro, a commute shed), zoom in. At national scale it would be a smudge.

Texas is the tightest state at {{text:tx_rate}}. The zoom shows where inside it: the core around our Irving campus, not the outer ring.

McKesson spec

  • Always a locator: a small US map, the frame in navy
  • The parent map's classes and colors, unchanged
  • Context outside the study area in sand
  • Small areas are noisy: hide any under {{text:min_n}} people

When a map is the wrong chart

  • Readers need to rank or compare precisely: a sorted bar
  • Five locations or fewer: a bar or a table

Choosing the geography

A real map is the default. Change the geography when it would hide what matters.

  • Small states matter as much as big ones: a tile grid
  • Data exists only by region: dissolve the states into regions
  • A few areas are too small to read: an enlarged inset
  • Detail smaller than a state: a zoom-in with a locator
  • Countries: an equal-area world map

Maps best practice

Maps are persuasive, so they must be honest about what the color means.

Rates, not counts. Shading headcount or postings just redraws where people live. Normalize: per candidate, per 1,000, a %. Show counts as bubbles.

Even, round classes. Three to five, no gaps or overlaps, open-ended at both ends.

One sequential hue. A rainbow makes some hues pop for no reason. Diverging only around a meaningful midpoint.

A quiet base. Light sand, thin white boundaries. For print, darken them to grey.

Maps best practice, continued

Four more, from workforce data in particular.

Bubbles by area. Scaling the radius makes a site twice as big look four times as big.

Label the few that matter. A number in every state is a table in the wrong shape, and the small states pile up.

Is it really geographic? Three hubs don't need a map of fifty states.

Aggregate, then map. Never plot home addresses. Map to state, metro or site, and drop anything under {{text:min_n}} people, in tooltips too.

Projections and zoom-ins

Where the shapes come from matters as much as the colors on them.

Equal-area for the world. Equal Earth, not Mercator or plain longitude and latitude: on a rate map, area is ink.

Albers for the US. Alaska and Hawaii as insets, said so in the footnote.

Zoom in, don't squint. County detail needs its own map, with a locator.

Keep the parent's scale. Use the same classes on the zoom; an automatic color range makes "dark" mean something new on every map.

Build maps

Choropleths are native in every tool; bubble, dot and zoom maps are easiest in Python or Plotly. Tested code and click-by-click steps are on the cookbook page.

maps.md

Before you send it

  • Shaded values are rates; counts are bubbles
  • 3–5 even classes; the key names the unit
  • Albers for the US, Equal Earth for the world
  • A zoom has a locator and the parent's classes
  • No area, site or cluster under {{text:min_n}} people
PowerPoint and Excel

Filled Map: Insert › Charts › Maps › Filled Map (online: Excel geocodes through Bing). It takes countries, states, counties or postal codes. Format Data Series: Map projection Albers for the US or Robinson for the world (never Mercator), Map area Only regions with data, which also zooms to a state's counties. Map a class-number column (1–5) to get binned shades; draw the key as small squares. Bubble and dot maps aren't native: build them in Python and insert the SVG.

Power BI

Azure Maps visual (your admin must enable it): Filled map layer for rates; Bubble layer with Range scaling Magnitude for counts. Bin colors with a SWITCH measure in Fill color › fx › Field value. The Shape map visual (preview) takes your own TopoJSON, such as counties or a tile grid. Suppress small groups with IF([Headcount] < 10, BLANK(), ...).

Python

The guide's maps use mckviz.geo: a small TopoJSON reader with the us-atlas and world-atlas files built in (states, world, a county subset), drawn with matplotlib; no geopandas. Or Plotly: go.Choropleth with fig.update_geos(scope="usa") (Albers USA) or projection_type="equal earth".

from mckviz import geo
ax = geo.canvas(fig)                               # axes in px
m = geo.Map("states-albers-10m.json", "states", (0, 0, 480, 300), key=geo.FIPS.get)
m.draw(ax, {s: ramp[c] for s, c in cls.items()}, ec="white", lw=0.6)
x, y = m.point(-96.80, 32.78)                      # a site, in Albers USA
HTML

Plotly with MCK_PLOTLY_TEMPLATE; for a zoom, go.Choropleth(geojson=…) and fitbounds="locations". Headline, subtitle and source go in the chart card's HTML. Tooltips may show n, but never for an area or site under {{text:min_n}}.

R

sf with tigris::shift_geometry() and coord_sf(crs = 5070) for Albers with insets; coord_sf(crs = "+proj=eqearth") for the world. scale_fill_manual() on the classes; bubbles with scale_size_area().
