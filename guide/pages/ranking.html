Ranking

Slope chart

Ranking charts show order: who comes first, who moved, and how far apart the items really are.

A slope chart joins each item's rank (or value) at two points in time. The eye reads the angle: steep lines are the movers, and crossings show who overtook whom. Use it for skills in job postings, sites or job families before and after a change.

McKesson spec

  • Five to ten items; one or two movers in blue
  • Names at both ends, in rank order; no legend
  • Rank 1 at the top; no axis or gridlines
  • Footnote what the rank is based on
  1. 1Insight headlineNames the movers and how far they moved.
  2. 2SubtitleWhat is ranked, where, which two years, and what 1 means.
  3. 3Movers in blueThe two the headline names, drawn last so they sit on top.
  4. 4Grey contextEvery other item, thinner.
  5. 5Names at both endsLeft in the starting order, right in the ending order.
  6. 6Period headersBold, above each column.
  7. 7The move, sizedA signed change beside the focus names.
  8. 8Rank 1 on topRanks evenly spaced; the numbers sit beside the names.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceWhat the rank measures.

Slope and bump variants

The same rules: grey context, one or two movers in color, names where the lines end.

When a slope is the wrong chart

  • The gaps matter more than the order: a sorted bar or a dot plot
  • More than about six periods, or the shape between them matters: a line chart
  • More than about ten items: show the top N, or use small multiples
  • One period only: a sorted bar

Slope and bump best practice

Crossing lines are the story, so make them easy to follow.

Highlight the movers. A color per line makes every crossing compete. Grey everything, then color the one or two the headline names.

Names at both ends. A legend sends the reader back and forth. Label each line where it starts and where it ends.

Rank 1 on top. A normal value axis puts first place at the bottom. Reverse it and drop the axis.

Ranks hide gaps. A swap of places looks like an even trade. When the size of the change matters, plot values.

Dot plot and dumbbell

A dot encodes position, not length, so the axis can start where the data starts. A dumbbell joins two dots per row, and the eye reads the gap.

Use a dot plot for scores far from zero (engagement, compa-ratio, proficiency) and a dumbbell for us vs. market, before vs. after, or plan vs. actual.

McKesson spec

  • Comparator grey, focus blue; orange only for worse
  • Light connector behind the dots
  • Signed gap at the row end; sorted by the gap
  • Round, non-zero range with light gridlines
  1. 1Insight headlineHow many rows fall short, and by how much.
  2. 2SubtitleBoth measures, the year and the unit.
  3. 3Grey comparator, blue focusMarket grey; McKesson blue.
  4. 4Worse in orangeThe roles 5% or more below market.
  5. 5A light connectorThe gap is what the eye reads.
  6. 6Every dot namedA one-line key above the rows: blue, orange and grey.
  7. 7Signed gapAt the row end, with a true minus; bold for the exceptions.
  8. 8Position, not lengthA round, non-zero range and light gridlines.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine the comparator.

Dot plot variants

Dots carry one, two or three values per row, on any scale that suits the data.

When a dot plot is the wrong chart

  • The size from zero matters (headcount, cost): a bar chart
  • More than two or three points in time: a line chart
  • A change in rank: a slope or bump chart
  • The spread across people: a distribution
  • Gaps smaller than a dot: plot the difference instead

Dot plot best practice

Dots are quiet marks. These four rules keep the comparison obvious.

Dots, not cut bars. When values sit far from zero, switch to dots. Never truncate a bar.

Connect the pair. A light connector shows the gap; name each dot type once, in a one-line key above the rows.

Grey comparator. Two strong colors leave the reader asking which one is us.

Sort by the gap. Alphabetical order hides the story. Put the largest shortfall first and write the gap at the row end.

Heat table

A table for looking things up, with one column shaded so the pattern shows before anyone reads a number.

Use it for scorecards and quarterly workforce packs: several measures in different units, which readers will copy into their own work. If there is one message about one measure, make the chart instead.

McKesson spec

  • Two shaded columns at most, blue by default
  • Horizontal hairlines only; no boxes or zebra stripes
  • Twelve rows and seven data columns at most
  • No cell under {{text:min_n}} people
  1. 1Insight headlineSays what the table shows.
  2. 2SubtitleScope, period, and how totals are built.
  3. 3Focus rowsBold navy across the whole row.
  4. 4One shaded columnBlue classes, light to dark; text flips to white on dark fills.
  5. 5Inline barsSize at a glance, from a common edge.
  6. 6Delta columnSigned; only the worse direction in orange.
  7. 7Units in the headersBare, right-aligned numbers, same decimals per column.
  8. 8Weighted total, lastUnder a navy rule; says what it covers.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceThe shading key and definitions.

Ranking

Heat table best practice

Formatting should point at the message, not decorate every cell.

Shade one or two columns. A color scale on every column is a Christmas tree: nothing stands out.

No traffic lights. Red, amber and green are status colors for workstreams, not shading for numbers, and fail for color-blind readers. Use blue classes; orange only when darker means worse.

Hairlines only. Boxes and zebra stripes add ink between the numbers. A header rule and light row lines are enough.

Weight the total. An average of the rates ({{text:rk_avg_simple}}) is not the rate for the group ({{text:rk_avg_weighted}}, weighted by headcount).

Native, editable charts and tables in every tool. Tested code and click-by-click steps are on the cookbook pages.

Before you send it

  • Rank 1 on top; names at both ends
  • Highlights only on what the headline names
  • Dots, not cut bars, for values far from zero
  • Units in table headers; two shaded columns at most
  • Totals weighted by headcount
  • Nothing ranked or shown under {{text:min_n}} people
PowerPoint and Excel

Slope and bump: Insert › Line with Markers; vertical axis Values in reverse order, bounds 0.5 to n + 0.5, labels none; context series grey 1 pt, focus 2.25 pt blue; Series Name labels on the first and last points. Dumbbell: XY scatter with custom horizontal error bars (no cap, 3 pt, light sand) as the connector. Heat table: clear the table style; bottom borders only; one conditional-formatting rule per blue class on the shaded column; data bars with Show Bar Only in a helper column.

Power BI

Bump: a Line chart on a RANKX measure with Y-axis › Invert range and series labels on. Dumbbell: the Deneb visual, or a line chart with markers and error bars. Heat table: a Table visual with Cell elements › Background color › fx › Rules on one measure. Blank small groups: IF([Headcount] < 10, BLANK(), [Rank]).

Python

matplotlib with mckviz; colors from mv.highlight, signed gaps from mv.signed, cell text from mv.text_on(fill).

colors = dict(zip(ranks, mv.highlight(ranks, focus=risers)))
for name, (r0, r1) in ranks.items():          # rank 1 at the top
    ax.plot([0, 1], [r0, r1], color=colors[name])
    ax.text(-0.05, r0, f"{name}  {r0}", ha="right", va="center")
    ax.text(1.05, r1, f"{r1}  {name}", ha="left", va="center")
ax.set_ylim(len(ranks) + 0.5, 0.5); ax.axis("off")
HTML

Plotly lines+markers+text with autorange: "reversed" for bumps; one connector trace with None breaks for dumbbells. Tables as real <table class="mck-table"> markup with is-focus, is-total and .bar.

R

geom_line() + geom_point() with scale_y_reverse(); geom_segment() under two geom_point() layers for dumbbells; gt with data_color(method = "bin") for heat tables.
