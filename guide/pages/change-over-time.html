Change over time

Line chart

Change-over-time charts show how something moves, and when it changed, at any time scale from weeks to decades.

A line connects values at equal time intervals, so the eye reads the trend rather than the individual amounts. Use it for monthly or quarterly attrition, hiring velocity, or a forecast.

McKesson spec

  • The series the headline names in blue; others grey
  • Data lines about twice the gridline weight
  • Names at the line ends, not in a legend
  • Shade the period that explains the change
  1. 1Insight headlineWhat changed, by how much, and why.
  2. 2SubtitleMetric, unit, scope and period.
  3. 3Focus lineBlue and heavier than the rest.
  4. 4Context linesGrey and thinner: there to compare against.
  5. 5The period that mattersLightly shaded and named.
  6. 6Direct labelsNames and latest values at the line ends, nudged apart.
  7. 7The change, sizedA short callout carries the headline number.
  8. 8Light scaffoldingA few thin gridlines; the unit lives in the subtitle.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine the metric.

Line chart variants

The same rules hold: one line in blue, names at the ends, light scaffolding.

When a line is the wrong chart

  • Only two to five periods, and the amounts matter: columns
  • How a total splits over time: stacked area
  • Discrete events of different sizes: a bubble timeline
  • Many series that tangle: small multiples
  • Two measures in different units: two aligned charts, never a dual axis

Line chart best practice

Four rules that make trends readable.

Highlight one line. A color per series doesn't show the trend; it makes the reader decode. Grey the background, color the message.

Lines for trends. Stacked columns hide how each part changes. When the trend is the point, draw lines.

No fill without a zero. Shading under a line turns it into an area chart, which implies a zero baseline.

Names at the line ends. A legend above the chart breaks the reading order.

Line chart best practice, continued

Four more, from HR and workforce data in particular.

Honest time spacing. Missing years still take up space. Use a real date axis, not evenly spaced labels.

Show gaps as gaps. A system change or survey break is not a straight line. Leave the gap and say why.

Weight with intent. Data lines about twice the gridlines; gridlines light, or gone.

Too many lines? Split them. More than four or five crossing lines become spaghetti. Use small multiples.

Stacked area chart

Layers stacked on each other show how a total splits over time, and how each part's share shifts.

Use it for a few parts that change gradually: how a role's hours divide between activities, or how the workforce mix moves. The layer on the baseline is the only one that reads precisely, so put the story there.

McKesson spec

  • Four or five layers at most
  • The story layer on the baseline, in blue; the rest ordered greys
  • Labels inside or at the right edge, not in a legend
  • Mark the event that explains the shift
  1. 1Insight headlineStart, end and the reason.
  2. 2SubtitleWhose time, what unit, which years.
  3. 3Story on the baselineBlue, so its growth reads directly.
  4. 4Grey contextOrdered from dark to light.
  5. 5Labels at the endName and latest share beside each layer.
  6. 6The event, markedA thin dashed line and a short name.
  7. 7Where it startedThe first value, so the change is explicit.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceHow time was measured.

Stacked area best practice

Areas are heavy marks. Keep them few, ordered and calm.

Stack, don't overlap. Areas drawn over each other hide what's behind. Stack them, or use lines.

Order the colors. Random colors make neighbors clash. Story layer blue on the baseline; the rest in ordered greys.

Volatile on top. A jumpy layer at the bottom makes every layer above it jump. Keep steady layers low.

Few layers. Four or five at most; fold the rest into "Other".

Bubble timeline

Events placed on a time axis, with bubble area showing size. It combines a timeline with a proportional-area chart.

Use it for discrete events of different sizes: announced layoffs in the talent market, reorganizations, hiring waves. Each category gets its own row.

McKesson spec

  • Bubble area, not diameter, proportional to value
  • One grey for all rows; the headline's row in blue
  • Largest bubbles drawn first, with white outlines
  • A size key with two or three reference circles
  1. 1Insight headlineWho stands out, by how much.
  2. 2SubtitleSays what bubble area means.
  3. 3One row per categorySorted by total, largest on top.
  4. 4Time along the bottomEven intervals, years labelled.
  5. 5Size keyReference circles; area ∝ value.
  6. 6Focus rowBlue; white outlines keep overlaps readable.
  7. 7The period that mattersShaded and named.
  8. 8Footnote and sourceWhat counts as an event.

Bubble timeline best practice

The rows already separate the categories; let size do the rest.

One color. Coloring each row repeats what the rows already say. Use grey, and blue for the headline's row.

Don't hide small bubbles. Draw the largest first, with white outlines.

Sort the rows. By total, or by a meaningful order, not alphabetically.

Show the scale. Without a size key, readers can only say "bigger".

Small multiples

The same small chart repeated for each group, on one shared scale. The eye compares shapes across panels instead of untangling crossing lines.

Use it when six or more groups each need their own trend: business units, regions, job families. In each panel, the panel's own line is blue and the others sit faintly behind it.

McKesson spec

  • One shared scale, stated once
  • Panels ordered by value or a natural order
  • Panel title = name + latest value
  • Axis labels only on the outer edge
  1. 1Insight headlineThe one panel that differs.
  2. 2SubtitleMetric, unit, period, once for all panels.
  3. 3The exceptionIn orange, because it is getting worse.
  4. 4Its own line in blueThe other units faint behind it, for context.
  5. 5Ordered by valueHighest latest value first.
  6. 6Shared scale0–12% in every panel.
  7. 7Title carries the numberName and latest value.
  8. 8Outer axes onlyLabels on the left column and bottom row.
  9. 9Footnote and sourceDefine the metric.

Small multiples best practice

Small multiples only work if every panel is read the same way.

One scale. Independent scales make a 0.5-point wobble look like the 5-point rise.

Context in every panel. The other units, faint behind each line, show whether a panel is unusual.

Order with meaning. By value, size or geography, not the alphabet.

Label the edges only. Repeated axes are clutter the eye reads six times.

Build change-over-time charts

Native, editable charts in every tool. Tested code and click-by-click steps are on the cookbook pages.

line.md · stacked-area.md · bubble-timeline.md · small-multiples.md

Before you send it

  • Time left to right, at true intervals
  • One line or layer in blue, matching the headline
  • Names at line ends; no legend
  • Forecasts dashed, labelled and with a range
  • Small multiples share one scale
PowerPoint and Excel

Line: Insert › Line; format the focus series 2.25 pt Focus blue, the rest 1.25 pt grey; delete the legend and add data labels to the last point only (Series Name + Value). Use a date axis so missing periods keep their space. Stacked area: Insert › 100% Stacked Area; story series first in Select Data; white 1 pt borders. Small multiples: build one chart, fix the axis bounds (Minimum/Maximum, not Auto), then copy it per group.

Power BI

Line chart with the focus series colored by measure; turn off the legend and use Series labels. Forecast bands as low/high measures shown with Error bars › Shaded area. Small multiples are built in: add the group to Small multiples and keep Y-axis › Shared.

Python

matplotlib with mckviz; mv.label_line_ends(ax) places and nudges end labels; mv.shade_period(ax, start, end, label) marks the period.

for fam, s in df.items():                 # one column per job family, one row per quarter
    ax.plot(x, s, label=fam, color=C.focus if fam == focus else C.context,
            lw=2.4 if fam == focus else 1.4)
mv.shade_period(ax, 6, 11, "After the reset")
mv.label_line_ends(ax, fmt="{:.1f}")      # names at the ends, nudged apart
HTML

Plotly scatter traces with mode: "lines", an annotation per line end, and a shape for the shaded period; facet_col (Plotly Express) for small multiples with shared axes.

R

geom_line() with mck_highlight() colors and geom_text() end labels; geom_area() for stacks; facet_wrap(~ unit) keeps a shared scale by default.
