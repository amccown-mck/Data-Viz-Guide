## Intent
Shows how to adapt data visualization for social media. Designers should think about the specific platform, because each one supports different formats and features and may reach a different audience. The page is a showcase of the house design language applied to broadcast/social content. It has no explicit do/don't examples.

## Guidance (paraphrased, every rule stated on the page)
- Consider the target platform when designing charts for social media.
- Each platform may support different formats and functions and may serve a different audience. Design with that in mind.
- The brand's visualization design language should carry over into broadcast/social content (the mockups are offered as examples of this).

## Examples marked Recommended
- None explicitly marked. All three are presented as positive examples:
  - A vertical mobile "story" format (phone mockup, 9:41, McKinsey & Co @mckinsey header with a close X). Serif headline on dark navy: "The market share for cryptocurrencies can grow a lot more." Below it are nested/adjacent proportional circles: a tiny bright-blue dot for Bitcoin ($66bn), white circles for all cryptocurrencies ($143bn) and Apple ($812bn), a large partial circle for all USD in circulation ($1,500bn), and a huge arc at the bottom for all money in circulation ($8,360bn). The screen ends with the prompt "Swipe up for more". It works because the full-screen vertical layout suits the format, the chart shows one striking comparison of scale, and the only highlight colour is on the subject.
  - A Twitter/X profile mockup showing a pinned tweet about mobility and electrification, with an attached image card. The card has a saturated blue background with a single large "75%" inside a thin progress ring and a short label (about the percentage of US workers who say they would work from home if they could). It has a small source line and logo. It works as a single bold KPI that is readable at thumbnail size.
  - A single tweet card: "Over the last three years, Los Angeles consumers have gained more purchasing power than any other city #DPP" with a link. The attached chart is a rank-change slope chart titled "A huge spike in wages awards Los Angeles the number one spot for domestic purchasing power", with the subtitle "Top five cities ranked by DPP*, 2015–2018". The 2015 ranks on the left are Zurich, Geneva, Luxembourg, Miami, Los Angeles. The 2018 ranks on the right are Los Angeles, Zurich, Miami, Geneva, Luxembourg. The Los Angeles line and markers are bright blue and every other line is dark/grey. It has a footnote defining DPP, a source line and the logo. It works because the tweet text states the insight and the chart has a single highlighted series that matches it.

## Visual specifics observed
- Color roles: dark navy is the background and context. White circles are comparison values. Bright blue (single accent) marks the subject (the Bitcoin dot, the Los Angeles line and markers). A saturated royal-blue card background is used for the KPI tile. Grey/dark thin lines are non-focal series.
- Proportional circles: no axes. Every circle is labelled directly with a bold value in $bn and a light-weight category label underneath. The largest circles bleed off the screen edges to suggest scale.
- Slope chart: numbered circular rank markers (1–5) on both sides, city names placed outside the markers, and year headers (2015, 2018) above each column. Thin connecting lines, with the highlight line in blue. No gridlines. The chart sits on a faint map-like texture background.
- KPI ring: a thin white ring arc around a large "75%" numeral, with a small left-aligned descriptor text.
- Typography: the story headline is a large serif in white. Values are bold sans and labels are light sans. The chart title in the tweet image is bold sans, with a smaller subtitle and a tiny footnote/source. The tweet text is a large sans.
- Sort: rank order from 1 to 5, top to bottom, on both sides of the slope chart.

## Annotation & narrative patterns
- Headlines state the insight ("…can grow a lot more", "A huge spike in wages awards Los Angeles the number one spot…").
- The social post text repeats the chart's key message, adds a hashtag (#DPP) and a short link.
- Chart images carry their own source line, footnote (* definition of DPP) and a small McKinsey & Company logo in the bottom corner, so they stand alone when shared.
- The story format uses a call to action, "Swipe up for more".
